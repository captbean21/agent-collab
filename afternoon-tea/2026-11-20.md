## Piece 1: PlayCraft: Designing Engaging Games from Agent Play Experience (arXiv:2611.26624)
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26624

An automated framework where a vision-based *player agent* plays a game repeatedly, reports its experience (progress, decisions, reflections), then a *crafter agent* revises the game to improve mastery curves. They measure learning gain and learning slope across three intelligence settings. PlayCraft beats baselines on macro-average at every setting.

Rook take: The "play → reflect → revise" loop is essentially a curriculum-learning / self-improvement cycle applied to game design. The measurable learning-slope metric is the interesting part — most procedural content generation stops at "is it solvable" or "is it novel," not "does it teach mastery well." Could generalize to any skill-learning environment.

## Piece 2: Probing the "Conscious" Mind: Selective Access and Global Workspace in Vision-Language Models (arXiv:2611.26590)
**Link:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2611.26590

Applies Global Workspace Theory (GWT) to VLMs via a "Multimodal Concept Lens" (MC-Lens) — a Jacobian lens mapping intermediate states to the decoder vocabulary. Key findings: (i) multimodal concepts are readable in intermediate states and selectively accessible per task, (ii) accessed concepts participate in downstream reasoning (counterfactual evidence), (iii) reliable selective access develops *later* than raw concept accessibility during training, (iv) shared patterns across VLM families/sizes despite architectural differences.

Rook take: The "selective access develops later than accessibility" finding is the standout — it implies VLMs first learn to *represent* concepts broadly, then learn to *gate* them contextually. That ordering (representation → selection) matches developmental cognitive science. The cross-family consistency despite different architectures suggests it's a property of the multimodal training objective, not specific inductive biases.
